Purdue University — Supplement Essay: Community

CheqIn's origin story and its connection to Purdue's EPICS program

Community Essay — Final Version

Before CheqIn was an app, it was Sunday mornings with my grandmother in India, teaching me for the fifth time how to make [FILL IN: a specific dish/tradition] without ever sounding tired of repeating herself — the kind of patience I didn't understand as love until I was older. That patience is also what my mom lost access to once we moved here: I'd catch her at 2 a.m. doing the math on the time difference, trying to decide if it was too early or too late to call. The distance wasn't really the problem. It was not knowing, day to day, whether her parent had eaten, taken medication, or had a bad morning, with no way to find out except a call that rarely landed at a good time for either of them.

I built CheqIn to protect the thing my grandparents had already taught me mattered most: the small, repeated proof that someone is paying attention. I trained the AI voice that runs the check-in to ask again when an answer trailed off, to wait instead of moving on, to sound like it had nowhere else to be — the same patience my grandmother showed me without ever making it feel like patience. What I didn't expect was that it wouldn't stay inside my family: friends heard about it, then their parents did, and now grandparents I've never met use it every day.

EPICS runs on that same model at Purdue: instead of textbook problems, student teams rapid-prototype real systems for real communities. It's multidisciplinary by design — pulling in business, computer science, and data analytics majors alongside engineers — because a project like CheqIn needs people who understand both how to build it and who it's for. That's the team I want to keep building with.
